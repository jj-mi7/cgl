Marking Scheme

✅ Correct Answer

+3 marks — same for both MCQ and TITA questions.

❌ Wrong MCQ

–1 mark. MCQs have 4 options; guessing blind has negative expected value.

✍️ TITA (Type-In-The-Answer)

No negative marking. You type the numeric/text answer instead of picking an option — so attempt every TITA you can even partially work out.

⏭️ Unattempted

0 marks, no penalty. Skipping a set you can't crack beats guessing blind on MCQs.

Section-wise Composition

📘 VARC (24 Qs)

~16 Qs from 4 Reading Comprehension passages (dense, abstract — philosophy, economics, science, culture). ~8 Qs of Verbal Ability: Para Jumbles, Para Summary, Odd Sentence Out, Para Completion — all TITA/no fixed options in recent years.

📊 DILR (22 Qs)

4–6 sets of 3–6 Qs each. Mixed DI+LR within a set is common now. Topics: tables, bar/line/pie charts, caselets, linear/circular arrangements, games & tournaments, Venn diagrams, scheduling, network/route puzzles.

🔢 QA (22 Qs)

Arithmetic dominates (35–40%): percentages, ratio, TSD, SI/CI, time & work, averages. Rest: Algebra, Number System, Geometry & Mensuration, and a few Modern Math (P&C, Probability, Sequences) questions.

Overall Exam-Day Strategy

  • 01
    Sections run in fixed order: VARC → DILR → QA You can't jump ahead or go back once a section's 40 minutes end. Pace each section on its own clock.
  • 02
    2–3 solid sets/passages beat attempting everything A 99-percentile VARC score often comes from 3 well-read passages + most VA, not from rushing all 4 passages shallowly. Same logic applies to DILR sets.
  • 03
    TITA questions are close to free value No negative marking — always leave a reasoned attempt on TITA even under time pressure, never leave a workable TITA blank.
  • 04
    Only attempt an MCQ if you can eliminate at least 2 of 4 options Expected value of a blind 1-in-4 guess is negative (+3×0.25 – 1×0.75 = –0.375). Two-option guesses are close to break-even.
  • 05
    Sectional cutoffs matter as much as overall score IIMs apply sectional cutoffs for shortlisting — a very strong QA score cannot fully compensate for a weak VARC or DILR attempt.
  • 06
    Scan before you solve, in DILR especially Spend the first 3–4 minutes previewing all sets, then commit to the 2–3 that look most tractable rather than solving in printed order.

Quick Memory Patterns

🟡 Square ending digits

A perfect square NEVER ends in 2, 3, 7, or 8. Use this to instantly eliminate options!

Valid endings: 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9
e.g. 482? → ends in 2 → NOT a perfect square ✗

🔵 Square of numbers ending in 5

For any number ending in 5: multiply the tens digit by (tens digit + 1), then append 25.

35² → 3×4 = 12 → append 25 → 1225 ✓
65² → 6×7 = 42 → append 25 → 4225 ✓
75² → 7×8 = 56 → append 25 → 5625 ✓

🟢 Cubes: Digital sum pattern

The digital sum of n³ follows a fixed cycle: 1, 8, 9, 1, 8, 9… (for n = 1,2,3,4…). Use this to verify cube values.

1³=1 → DS=1 | 2³=8 → DS=8
3³=27 → DS=9 | 4³=64 → DS=10→1

🔴 (a+b)² shortcut

Split the number near a round base.
(a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b²

98² = (100-2)² = 10000 - 400 + 4 = 9604
52² = (50+2)² = 2500 + 200 + 4 = 2704

🟡 Square of numbers ending in 1

For (10x+1)²: take x², then 2x, then 1 — place value with carries: x² | 2x | 1.

61² → x=6: 36 | 12 | 1 → 3600+120+1 = 3721 ✓
91² → x=9: 81 | 18 | 1 → 8100+180+1 = 8281 ✓

🔵 Squares of Repunits (11, 111…)

Squares of numbers made entirely of 1s follow a rising-then-falling digit pattern.

11² = 121
111² = 12321
1111² = 1234321

🟢 Perfect square — second-last digit rule

If a perfect square ends in 6, its second-last (tens) digit is always odd. For any other ending digit, the second-last digit is always even.

16 (4²) → ends 6, tens digit 1 (odd) ✓
36 (6²) → ends 6, tens digit 3 (odd) ✓
25 (5²) → ends 5, tens digit 2 (even) ✓

🟡 % increase then decrease

If a value increases by a% then decreases by a%, the net change is always a loss:

Net % = –(a²/100)
e.g. +10% then –10% = –1%

🔵 Two successive % changes

Net % when changed by a% then b%:

Net = a + b + (ab/100)
e.g. +20% then +10%
= 20+10+(200/100) = +32%

🟢 X% of Y = Y% of X

This symmetry is extremely handy. Swap when the reverse is easier to compute.

8% of 25 = 25% of 8 = 2
4% of 75 = 75% of 4 = 3

🔴 Percentage point vs %

If something goes from 40% to 50%, it rose by 10 percentage points, but by 25% relatively (10/40 × 100).

CAT loves this distinction in DI questions!

LCM & HCF — Formulas & Tricks

📌 LCM × HCF = Product of two numbers

LCM(a,b) × HCF(a,b) = a × b e.g. LCM(12,18)=36, HCF=6 36 × 6 = 12 × 18 = 216 ✓ ⚠️ Works only for TWO numbers, NOT three! HCF always ≤ both numbers. LCM always ≥ both numbers.

🔑 HCF of fractions

HCF of fractions = HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators LCM of fractions = LCM of numerators / HCF of denominators e.g. HCF of 2/3 and 4/9: HCF(2,4)=2; LCM(3,9)=9 → HCF = 2/9

⚙️ Repeated division method

Step 1: Divide larger by smaller. Step 2: Divide previous divisor by remainder. Step 3: Repeat until remainder = 0. Last divisor = HCF. HCF(48, 18): 48÷18 rem 12 → 18÷12 rem 6 → 12÷6 rem 0 → HCF=6

⚠️ CAT Trap — Word Problems

"Max tiles to cover floor l×b" → HCF(l, b) "Containers that exactly hold X and Y litres" → HCF "First time two bells ring together" → LCM "Largest number that divides a, b, c exactly" → HCF "Smallest number divisible by a, b, c" → LCM

📌 HCF/LCM — Number Representation

If HCF(a,b) = h, the numbers can be written as ha and hb (a,b co-prime). Then LCM(a,b) = hab. ⚠️ Also: HCF of two numbers is always a factor of BOTH their sum and their difference. e.g. HCF(12,18)=6 → sum=30 (÷6 ✓), diff=6 (÷6 ✓)

Remainders & Important Properties

REMAINDER RULES (CAT PYQ Favourites):
Remainder when 2¹⁰ ÷ 3 → Pattern: 2¹=2, 2²=4, 2³=8... remainder with 3 cycles: 2,1,2,1... → 2¹⁰ → even power → rem=1
Any number² – 1 is divisible by 8 if the number is odd. (CAT PYQ repeated fact)
Remainder of n! ÷ p (where p≤n is prime) = 0. e.g. 7! ÷ 5 → remainder = 0
The product of n consecutive integers is always divisible by n!. e.g. 5×6×7 (3 consecutive) is divisible by 3!=6 → 210÷6=35 ✓
Last n digits (CRT method): since 10ⁿ = 2ⁿ × 5ⁿ, to find the last n digits of a number, find its remainder mod 2ⁿ and mod 5ⁿ separately, then combine the two using the Chinese Remainder Theorem.
e.g. Last 2 digits of 3⁴⁷: mod 4 → 3≡–1, so 3⁴⁷≡(–1)⁴⁷≡3 (mod 4). mod 25 → order of 3 is 20, 47 mod 20=7, 3⁷=2187≡12 (mod 25). Combine: need x≡3(mod4), x≡12(mod25) → x=87. So 3⁴⁷ ends in 87.

UNIT DIGIT PATTERNS (Power Cycles):
Powers of 2: unit digit cycles → 2,4,8,6,2,4,8,6... (period 4)
Powers of 3: 3,9,7,1,3,9,7,1... (period 4)
Powers of 7: 7,9,3,1,7,9,3,1... (period 4)

Primality Test — Quick Method

HOW TO CHECK IF A NUMBER IS PRIME (√n method):
Find √n (round to nearest whole number). Check divisibility only by primes from 2, 3, 5, 7… up to √n. If none divide n exactly, n is prime — no need to check beyond √n.
e.g. Is 97 prime? √97 ≈ 9.8 → check 2, 3, 5, 7 only → none divide 97 → 97 is prime.
e.g. Is 91 prime? √91 ≈ 9.5 → check 2, 3, 5, 7 → 7 × 13 = 91 → not prime (common CAT trap number).

6n ± 1 RULE (fast filter, not a full proof):
Every prime number greater than 3 can be written in the form 6n ± 1 (n = 1, 2, 3…). Handy to shortlist candidates fast, but not every 6n±1 number is prime — still verify with the √n method.
e.g. n=1 → 5, 7 (both prime). n=2 → 11, 13 (both prime). n=4 → 23 (prime), 25 = 5×5 (not prime, despite fitting the form).

All Algebraic Identities

BASIC SQUARES & PRODUCTS:
(a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
(a–b)² = a² – 2ab + b²
(a+b)² + (a–b)² = 2(a²+b²)
(a+b)² – (a–b)² = 4ab
(a+b)(a–b) = a² – b²
(a+b+c)² = a²+b²+c²+2ab+2bc+2ca

CUBES:
(a+b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³ = a³+b³+3ab(a+b)
(a–b)³ = a³ – 3a²b + 3ab² – b³ = a³–b³–3ab(a–b)
a³+b³ = (a+b)(a²–ab+b²) = (a+b)³–3ab(a+b)
a³–b³ = (a–b)(a²+ab+b²) = (a–b)³+3ab(a–b)

GOLDEN RULE — IF a+b+c=0:
a³+b³+c³ = 3abc ← appears in almost EVERY CAT paper
Also: a²+b²+c² = –2(ab+bc+ca) when a+b+c=0

FACTORING SHORTCUT (a²–b²):
999²–998² = (999+998)(999–998) = 1997 × 1 = 1997 (instant!)
Any diff of squares Q: factor as (a+b)(a–b) first.

CAT-Style Algebra Patterns

🔑 x + 1/x = k Pattern

If x + 1/x = k, then: x² + 1/x² = k² – 2 x³ + 1/x³ = k³ – 3k = k(k²–3) x⁴ + 1/x⁴ = (x²+1/x²)² – 2 = (k²–2)²–2 CAT TRAP: If x + 1/x = 2, then x = 1. Substitute x=1 to verify any expression.

🔑 x – 1/x = k Pattern

If x – 1/x = k, then: x² + 1/x² = k² + 2 x³ – 1/x³ = k³ + 3k = k(k²+3) Verify: if x – 1/x = 0, then x = 1 or –1. Substitute to cross-check options instantly.

🔑 a + b + c = 0 Trick

Given: a+b+c = 0 → a³+b³+c³ = 3abc Corollary: (a+b) = –c, (b+c) = –a, (a+c) = –b Use these to simplify complex fractions! Example: (a+b)(b+c) = (–c)(–a) = ac Any expression with "a+b+c=0" given → look for the 3abc pattern first.

⚠️ Value Substitution Method

For identity Qs: put a=1, b=1 (or simple values). LHS and RHS must match. Eliminates wrong options. Also: for equations like x²+y²=xy, put x=y=0 or x=y=1 to check options. Never substitute 0 if division by variable exists.

Ratio & Proportion

📐 Basic Rules

a:b :: c:d → a×d = b×c (Product of means = product of extremes) Compounded ratio of a:b and c:d = ac:bd Duplicate ratio of a:b = a²:b² Triplicate ratio of a:b = a³:b³ Sub-duplicate ratio of a:b = √a:√b Inverse ratio of a:b = b:a

🔀 Dividing in Ratio

Total T divided in ratio a:b:c → Part A = T × a/(a+b+c) Part B = T × b/(a+b+c) Part C = T × c/(a+b+c) e.g. Rs 720 in ratio 3:4:5 → A=720×3/12=180; B=240; C=300

⚡ Proportion Types

DIRECT proportion: y = kx (both increase together) INVERSE proportion: y = k/x (one up, other down) FOURTH proportional: a:b::c:x → x = bc/a THIRD proportional: a:b::b:x → x = b²/a MEAN proportional between a,b: x = √(ab)

⚠️ Partnership Profit Split

If time is same: split profit in ratio of capitals. If time differs: Profit ∝ Capital × Time A: Rs 6000 for 6 months; B: Rs 8000 for 5 months A : B = 6000×6 : 8000×5 = 36000 : 40000 = 9:10 Total profit 19000 → A gets 9000, B gets 10000

Mixture & Alligation — The Arrow Method

🎯 Alligation Formula

Two things mixed at prices/concentrations C₁ and C₂ to get mean price/concentration Cm: Ratio of quantities = (C₂ – Cm) : (Cm – C₁) Draw a cross (X): C₁ C₂ \ / Cm / \ (C₂–Cm) (C₁–Cm) ← ratio of mixing

💧 Mixture Problems

DILUTION: Milk of volume V, concentration c₁. Replace x litres with water n times: Final conc = c₁ × (1 – x/V)ⁿ e.g. 20L milk, 4L removed and replaced 3 times: Final = 20×(16/20)³ = 20×(4/5)³ = 20×0.512 = 10.24L

⚡ Average + Mixture Combined

Average price of mix = (n₁×p₁ + n₂×p₂) / (n₁+n₂) Alligation gives the RATIO directly: e.g. Rs 12/kg and Rs 18/kg mixed to get Rs 14/kg: (18–14) : (14–12) = 4:2 = 2:1 → 2 parts of cheap + 1 part of expensive

⚠️ Averages — Key Formulas

Average = Sum / Count New avg after adding element x to n numbers: New avg = (old avg × n + x) / (n+1) If avg of n consecutive numbers = middle term Sum of 1 to n = n(n+1)/2 Sum of squares 1²+2²+…+n² = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6 Sum of cubes 1³+2³+…+n³ = [n(n+1)/2]² Sum of first n odd natural numbers = n² (e.g. n=5: 1+3+5+7+9=25=5² ✓) Sum of first n even natural numbers = n(n+1) (e.g. n=5: 2+4+6+8+10=30=5×6 ✓)

Similarity, Congruence & Key Theorems

🔺 Similar Triangles (AA/SAS/SSS)

If △ABC ~ △DEF (ratio k): Corresponding sides proportional: AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD = k Ratio of areas = k² (square of side ratio) Ratio of perimeters = k Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT / Thales): If DE || BC in △ABC, then AD/DB = AE/EC

📏 Mid-Point & Apollonius Theorem

Mid-Point Theorem: Line joining midpoints of two sides of △ is parallel to 3rd side and = half of it. Apollonius Theorem (Median): In △ABC, if D is midpoint of BC: AB² + AC² = 2(AD² + BD²) This is the most important theorem for median questions in CAT!

📐 Angle Bisector Theorem

If AD bisects ∠A in △ABC: BD/DC = AB/AC (Bisector divides opposite side in ratio of adjacent sides) If internal bisector of ∠A meets BC at D: BD/DC = c/b (where b = AC, c = AB)

⚠️ Right Triangle in Semicircle

Angle in a semicircle = 90° (Thales' theorem) If AB is diameter, ∠ACB = 90° always. This is the most commonly used circle property in CAT. If altitude is drawn from right angle to hypotenuse: altitude² = product of the two segments

Simple & Compound Interest

📈 Simple Interest (SI)

SI = P × R × T / 100 Amount = P + SI = P(1 + RT/100) P = 100 × SI / (R × T) R = 100 × SI / (P × T) T = 100 × SI / (P × R)

📊 Compound Interest (CI)

Amount A = P(1 + R/100)ⁿ CI = A – P For 2 years: CI = P[(1+R/100)² – 1] CI 2yr shortcut = 2×SI_1yr + SI_1yr × R/100 Half-yearly: A = P(1 + R/200)^(2n) Quarterly: A = P(1 + R/400)^(4n)

💡 CI vs SI Difference

For 2 years: CI – SI = P(R/100)² For 3 years: CI – SI = P(R/100)²(3 + R/100) If SI for 2 yrs = X, then CI for 2 yrs = X + X²/(4P) Most CAT CI questions use 2-year formula!

🔑 Rule of 72

Time to double money at R% CI ≈ 72/R years e.g. At 8% CI: doubles in ≈ 72/8 = 9 years At 12% CI: doubles in ≈ 6 years Population growth: same formula as CI. If population increases R% annually: Population after n years = P₀(1 + R/100)ⁿ

Time & Work

⚙️ LCM Method (Fastest)

A does work in 'a' days, B in 'b' days. Take LCM of a, b as total work (units). A's rate = LCM/a units/day B's rate = LCM/b units/day Together rate = A's + B's Time = Total work / Combined rate e.g. A=12 days, B=15 days LCM=60; A=5/day, B=4/day; Together=9/day Time = 60/9 = 6⅔ days ✓

⚙️ Standard Formulas

A + B together: T = ab/(a+b) A alone, after A+B together for t days: Remaining = 1 – t(1/a + 1/b); then A alone finishes it. If A is twice as efficient as B: A takes half the time of B. Work × Rate = 1 (for 1 unit of work) Men × Days = constant (same work) Men × Days × Hours = constant

🚿 Pipes & Cisterns

Filling pipe (A): + (positive work) Emptying pipe (B): – (negative work) Net rate = filling rate – emptying rate If A fills in 'a' hrs, B empties in 'b' hrs: Net fill time = ab/(b–a) [if b > a, tank fills] CAT common: "Both opened simultaneously..." → Use LCM of a and b as total capacity.

⚠️ Efficiency Variations

Men–Days–Hours formula: M₁D₁H₁/W₁ = M₂D₂H₂/W₂ e.g. 6 men, 5 days, 8 hrs = same work as 4 men, ? days, 6 hrs: 6×5×8/1 = 4×D×6/1 D = 240/24 = 10 days ✓

Digital Sum — The MCQ Eliminator

Not for solving — for verifying answers in seconds. Ideal for MCQ-format CAT.

Definition

What is Digital Sum?

Repeatedly add all digits of a number until you get a single digit. That single digit is the digital sum.

Example: 2,347 → 2+3+4+7 = 16 → 1+6 = 7 → Digital Sum = 7
Example: 239 → 2+3+9 = 14 → 1+4 = 5 → Digital Sum = 5
Key Rules

Rule 1 — Skip 9s (and groups adding to 9)

Any digit 9, or any group of digits that sum to 9, can be ignored. This speeds up the process enormously.

239 → skip 9 → 2+3 = 5 ✓ (same result, much faster)
369 → skip 9, skip 3+6 → Digital Sum = 9 (or 0)

Rule 2 — Digital Sum of the Answer = Digital Sum of the Operation

LHS digital sum must equal RHS digital sum. If an option doesn't match, it's wrong.

Q: 13 + 12 = ? → DS of 13+12 = 1+3+1+2 = 7
Options: 24(DS=6), 25(DS=7)✓, 26(DS=8) → Answer = 25

Rule 3 — Works for Multiplication & Division too

DS(A × B) = DS(A) × DS(B) → reduce to single digit.

Q: 24 × 13 = ? → DS(24)=6, DS(13)=4, 6×4=24 → DS=6
Correct answer must have digital sum 6. (Actual = 312, DS=6 ✓)

Rule 4 — Negative Digital Sum: Add 9

If DS comes out negative (in subtraction), add 9 to get the correct digital sum.

DS of (12 - 15) → DS=3, DS=6 → 3–6 = –3 → –3+9 = 6
Check: –3 mod 9 = 6

    Memorise tables to 30, squares to 30, cubes to 20 Saves 15–20 seconds per question, which compounds across 22 questions in a 40-minute section.

🔢 Number Series — Pattern Types

A sequence of numbers following a rule. You must find the missing/wrong term. The key is identifying the pattern type.
The 7 Main Patterns
1. ARITHMETIC (constant difference): 3, 7, 11, 15, 19 → +4 each time
2. GEOMETRIC (constant ratio): 2, 6, 18, 54 → ×3 each time
3. SQUARE SERIES: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 → 1², 2², 3², 4², 5²

POSITION SHIFT: D, G, J, M → +3 positions each (D=4, G=7, J=10, M=13)
REVERSE ALPHABET: Z, Y, X, W → –1 each (Z=26, Y=25…)
SKIP PATTERN: A, C, E, G → every other letter (vowel series)
GROUP PATTERN: ABC, DEF, GHI → groups of 3 consecutive letters
MIXED: A2Z, C4X, E6V → +2 letters, +2 numbers, –2 letters

TRICK — The EJOTY rule memorise: E=5, J=10, O=15, T=20, Y=25
This lets you instantly get positions without counting from A.
⚡ Always write numerical positions below letters. Never try to solve letter series by "feeling" — convert to numbers and treat as a number series.

🧭 Direction Sense — 5 Rules

A person walks in various directions — find final direction from start, distance, or displacement. Always draw it on paper.
Rules & Examples
RULE 1: Right turn = 90° clockwise. Left turn = 90° anti-clockwise.
RULE 2: U-turn = 180° turn (you now face the opposite direction).
RULE 3: Displacement = straight-line distance from start to end point.
Use Pythagoras: if 3 km East then 4 km North → displacement = √(9+16) = 5 km NE.
RULE 4: Shadow in morning (sunrise) falls to the WEST. Shadow in evening falls to the EAST.
RULE 5: If facing North → Right = East, Left = West, Behind = South.

EXAMPLE: Start facing North. Walk 5 km North, turn right, walk 3 km, turn right, walk 5 km.
→ End up 3 km East of start, facing South.
⚡ Draw a compass (N/S/E/W cross) at the top of your paper. Mark every step as an arrow. Never track direction mentally alone.

Syllogism & Venn Diagrams

📐 Syllogism — The Venn Diagram Method

Two or more statements → test conclusions. The definitive method: draw all possible Venn diagrams for the statements, then check each conclusion.
Statement Types & What They Mean
"All A are B" → A circle fully inside B circle.
"No A is B" → A and B circles completely separate (no overlap).
"Some A are B" → A and B circles partially overlapping.
"Some A are not B" → part of A circle is outside B circle.

CONCLUSION TEST:
→ If conclusion is true in ALL possible diagrams → "Definitely follows"
→ If true in SOME diagrams only → "Does not definitely follow" (unless "possibly")
→ If false in all diagrams → "Does not follow"

EXAMPLE: All cats are animals. Some animals are dogs.
Conclusion 1: "Some cats are dogs" → Does NOT follow (cats and dogs may not overlap).
Conclusion 2: "Some animals are cats" → FOLLOWS (since all cats are animals).
⚡ The #1 mistake: concluding what is "likely" vs what is "definite". CAT demands logical certainty. "Some" does NOT mean "all", and "No A is B" never means "No B is A" is a conclusion — it already implies that, but students miss it.

🪑 Seating Arrangement — Linear & Circular

Persons seated in a row or circle — decode positions from clues. Most time-consuming topic. Attempt only after all easy Qs are done.
Step-by-Step Method
LINEAR SEATING:
Step 1: Draw boxes for each position (e.g., 6 boxes for 6 persons).
Step 2: Use absolute clues first ("A is at one end").
Step 3: Use relative clues next ("B is 2 positions right of A").
Step 4: Use elimination clues last ("C is not adjacent to D").

CIRCULAR SEATING:
Step 1: Fix ONE person at the top (12 o'clock). This is your reference point.
Step 2: "Facing centre" = left is anti-clockwise, right is clockwise.
Step 3: "Immediate left" of person X = the person sitting anti-clockwise from X.

EXAMPLE CLUE SET (Linear, 5 people A–E):
A is at one end. B is between C and D. E is at the other end. C is between A and B.
→ A – C – B – D – E ✓
⚡ CAT seating/arrangement sets usually involve 5–8 entities with several interlocking clues across 3–5 questions. Spend the first 2–3 minutes just building the base framework from absolute clues before touching individual questions — and if you can't place most entities within ~5 minutes, that set is a strong skip candidate.

📊 Ranking Formula

If rank from top = R, rank from bottom = R', total = N:

N = R + R' – 1

Q: Ravi is 8th from top, 5th from bottom. Total?
N = 8 + 5 – 1 = 12 students ✓

Q: 40 students, Priya is 14th from left. Rank from right?
40 – 14 + 1 = 27th ✓

🕐 Clock Angle Formula

Angle between hour and minute hands at H hours M minutes:

θ = |30H – 5.5M|
If >180°, subtract from 360°.

Q: Angle at 3:20?
= |30×3 – 5.5×20| = |90 – 110| = 20° ✓

Coincide: hands meet every 65 5/11 minutes.

    Spend the first 3–4 minutes scanning all sets before solving any DILR sets vary hugely in difficulty. Reading the setup (not the questions) for each set tells you which 2–3 are tractable.
  • 02
    Prioritise tables, bar/line graphs, and simple arrangements These make up roughly half of a typical DILR section and are generally the fastest, most reliable marks.
  • 03
    Aim for 2–3 sets at 90%+ accuracy, not 5–6 sets attempted shakily In DILR, accuracy on fewer sets consistently outperforms breadth. Many 99-percentile scorers fully solve just 2–3 sets.
  • 04
    If you're not through the setup in ~5 minutes, abandon the set Sunk-cost thinking on a hard set is the single biggest score-killer in DILR. Move to your next-best option.
  • 05
    Number Series: always find the difference series first Subtract consecutive terms — a constant difference means arithmetic, a multiplying difference means geometric-of-differences. This solves most series-type sub-questions inside larger sets quickly.
  • 06
    Blood relations: always draw the family tree Even for a short one-line clue. Diagramming prevents misreading chained relations like "A is the son of B's father."
  • 07
    Direction sense: draw a compass before the first clue Plot each movement as you read rather than tracking direction mentally — this is a common source of careless errors under time pressure.
  • 08
    Syllogism / Venn logic: test every possible diagram, not just the obvious one For "Some A are B," multiple diagrams are valid (partial overlap, A inside B, B inside A). A conclusion must hold in ALL valid diagrams to definitely follow.
  • 09
    Ranking formula: N = R₁ + R₂ – 1 Covers rank-from-top + rank-from-bottom → total-count questions that often appear as a quick sub-part of a larger arrangement set.
  • 10
    Circular arrangements: fix one entity as your reference point "Right" = clockwise, "left" = anti-clockwise when facing the centre. This removes rotational ambiguity immediately.
  • 11

  • 12
    Venn diagram sets: shade and label every region before calculating "Only A," "A and B not C," "all three" — draw three overlapping circles, label each region, then fill in numbers systematically from the given clues.
  • 13
    Caselets: extract numbers into your own table first Don't re-read the paragraph for every question — convert it into a mini data table once, then answer from that table.
  • 14
    Track one variable at a time in complex multi-constraint puzzles Don't try to satisfy every clue simultaneously — place the most restrictive/absolute clues first, then work relative clues around them.
  • 15
    Networks/routes: always sketch the diagram, never track mentally Distances and connections compound fast — a visual graph prevents the most common calculation errors.
  • 16
    TITA sub-questions inside a DILR set carry no negative marking Once you've built the underlying table/diagram for a set, attempt every TITA question from it — the setup cost is already paid.

    Solve 2–3 DILR sets daily, mixing DI-heavy and LR-heavy sets Daily exposure builds pattern recognition — many high scorers report going from ~50% to ~80% set accuracy over 2–3 months of consistent practice.

VARC — Verbal Ability & Reading Comprehension

24 Qs · 40 min · ~16 RC (4 passages) + ~8 VA (Para Jumbles, Summary, Odd Sentence, Completion)

Reading Comprehension — Approach

📖 How to Read a CAT RC Passage

CAT passages are dense — philosophy, economics, ecology, science history, culture. The goal isn't speed-reading every word; it's extracting structure and argument on the first pass.
The 4-Step Read
1. SKIM first and last paragraphs — these usually carry the main claim and conclusion.
2. Note the AUTHOR'S TONE and stance (critical, neutral, advocating) — many questions hinge on this.
3. Track TRANSITION WORDS (however, therefore, whereas, in contrast) — they mark where the argument turns.
4. Don't stop to fully "solve" hard sentences on the first read — mark them and return only if a question needs that detail.
⚡ Read the passage before the questions, not after. Question-first reading fragments comprehension and costs more time overall on 500+ word CAT passages.

🎯 Set Selection

Skim all 4 passages in the first few minutes. Attempt the 2–3 you find clearest thoroughly (aim for 90%+ accuracy) rather than rushing all 4 shallowly.

🧭 Inference vs Opinion

CAT RC answers must be supported by the passage — not by what's generally true or what you personally believe. Eliminate options that are "outside the passage," even if factually correct.

📏 Extreme Language Trap

Options with "always," "never," "completely," "only" are frequently wrong unless the passage uses equally absolute language. Moderate-language options are often safer.

⏱️ Stamina Building

Practice sustained focused reading (20 → 30 → 40 minutes over weeks) with zero distractions — mid-passage attention drop is a bigger score-killer than vocabulary gaps.

Verbal Ability — Question Types

🔀 Para Jumbles (TITA)

4–5 sentences given out of order — type the correct sequence. No fixed options in recent papers, so the elimination logic has to be airtight.
Method
1. Find the OPENER — the sentence that introduces the topic without relying on a pronoun/reference from elsewhere.
2. Find the CLOSER — often summarises, concludes, or has a forward-looking/resolving tone.
3. Use LINKING CLUES — pronouns (he, it, this), transition words (however, therefore), and repeated nouns to chain sentences together.
4. Build pairs first (sentences that clearly link), then slot the pairs into the full sequence.
⚡ Read the full jumbled set once before touching any sentence — grasp the overall topic first, then hunt for the opener.

📝 Para Summary

A short paragraph is given — pick (or type) the option that best captures its core argument, not just a topic restatement.
Elimination Rules
REJECT options that only cover PART of the paragraph (too narrow).
REJECT options that add information NOT in the paragraph (over-reaching).
REJECT options that just restate the topic without the paragraph's actual claim.
ACCEPT the option that compresses the main idea + the author's takeaway in the fewest words.
⚡ Write a one-line mental summary yourself before reading the options — this stops you from being swayed by a well-worded but inaccurate option.

❓ Odd Sentence Out / Para Completion

Odd Sentence Out: one of 4–5 sentences breaks the logical flow — identify it. Para Completion: pick the sentence that best completes a paragraph.
Approach
Read all sentences together first to get the paragraph's single central theme.
The odd sentence usually shifts topic, tone, or introduces an unrelated idea/example.
For completion, the right sentence continues the SAME argument direction — check what the previous sentence sets up (a contrast, an example, a consequence).
⚡ Don't pick the "most interesting" sentence — pick the one that's a logical misfit, even if individually well-written.

  • 08

Tips & Tricks — Reading List, Books & Exam-Day Playbook

How toppers actually structure daily prep, what to read, which books to pick, and the last-48-hours checklist.

📰 Daily Reading Habit

📖 Read The Hindu — Editorial & Op-Ed Pages Daily

This is the single most recommended non-book habit across CAT toppers and coaching institutes. The Hindu's editorial and opinion pages match CAT RC's tone, sentence complexity, and subject range (economics, policy, science, culture) far better than casual reading.
How to Read It for CAT, Not Just for News
1. Read the EDITORIAL (not the front-page news) — it's argumentative prose, structurally closest to a CAT RC passage.
2. After reading, write a ONE-LINE summary of the author's core argument from memory — this is literally Para Summary practice.
3. Note new vocabulary in context (not from a word list) — CAT tests word usage inside dense passages, not standalone meanings.
4. Once a week, take 4–5 opinion pieces and jumble their paragraphs yourself (or with a study partner) — self-made Para Jumbles.
⚡ 20–30 minutes a day, every day, from the start of your prep — this compounds far more than cramming RC passages only in the final months.

🗓️ A Realistic Prep Timeline

Months 1–3: Foundation

NCERT/basics for weak QA topics, start The Hindu editorial daily, learn one DILR set type per week, read 1 RC passage/day from old CAT papers.

Months 4–6: Application

Level 1–2 questions from your chosen books. 2–3 DILR sets/day across varied topics. 2 RC passages + 5 VA questions/day. Start sectional mocks.

Months 7 – D-Day: Strategy

Full-length mocks (1–2/week, more closer to exam), tougher previous-year questions, timed DILR set-selection drills, and error-log review after every mock.

Throughout: Mocks Are Non-Negotiable

Mock tests and previous-year papers matter as much as any book — they're the only way to practice real set-selection and time-pressure decisions.

🎯 General CAT Tips & Tricks

  • 01
    Analyse every mock beyond just the score Track set-selection quality, time per question, and error type (concept gap / calculation slip / mis-read) — the review teaches more than the attempt itself.
  • 02
    Don't chase every book — finish one per section Depth on a single well-chosen book beats shallow exposure to five. Use extra books only for a specific weak topic.
  • 03
    Build reading stamina progressively 20 minutes of focused reading in week 1, building to a full 40-minute distraction-free VARC simulation by the final months.
  • 04
    Solve DILR sets daily, not in occasional bursts Pattern recognition across arrangements, tables, and puzzles builds from consistent daily exposure, not last-minute cramming.
  • 05
    Simulate real exam conditions in your final 2 months Fixed 40-minute sectional windows, no switching back, no phone — train the exact constraint you'll face on exam day.
  • 06
    Sectional cutoffs mean you can't ignore a weak section A strong QA score won't offset a weak VARC attempt in most IIM shortlisting criteria — balance your practice across all three.
  • 07
    Keep a single error-log notebook across all three sections Note the question type, why you got it wrong, and the correct approach. Revisit this notebook weekly, not just before the exam.
  • 08
    TITA questions are close to free value everywhere in the paper No negative marking — in VARC, DILR, and QA alike, always submit a reasoned attempt rather than leaving it blank.

🏆 EXAM DAY STRATEGY — Read This First

Fixed order: VARC (40 min) → DILR (40 min) → QA (40 min). Never spend more than ~2 min stuck on one MCQ. Attempt MCQs only if you can eliminate at least 2 of 4 options — a blind guess has negative expected value (+3×0.25 – 1×0.75 = –0.375). Always attempt workable TITA questions — zero penalty. In VARC and DILR, prioritise depth on 2–3 passages/sets over shallow coverage of all of them.
